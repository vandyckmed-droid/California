The compression ceiling

Can 2,280 stocks be represented by a small set of industry-ETF bets? The data says no — and the machinery already in the product does the job roughly twice as well.

2026-08-28 756 sessions 2,280 stocks · 75 candidate ETFs npm run labs:etf-basis
Recommendation

Do not change Cali. An ETF basis represents 28% of the universe; the correlation grouping already in the product covers 48–59% at ten times the granularity.

The compression that does exist is real and worth knowing about — but it lives in about ten rate-sensitive and commodity-linked groups, which is roughly what ETF River already displays.

01 — MethodRemoving the market is the whole ballgame

Every series is residualised against SPY before anything is compared. Without that step the study cannot say anything, because two U.S. equity ETFs are correlated for the trivial reason that both are U.S. equities.

The size of the effect settles it. Across 2,775 candidate pairs, median |ρ| falls from 0.43 raw to 0.15 on market residuals. Similarity measured on raw returns would have found "redundancy" everywhere and produced a confidently wrong answer.

The candidate library carries its own falsifier

75 candidates include 13 pairs that are near-certainly the same bet in different wrappers — SMH/SOXX, XBI/IBB, ITA/XAR, GDX/GDXJ, RWR/VNQ and so on. Without a known answer in the data there is no way to tell a working redundancy method from a merely plausible one. The method merged 12 of the 13.

The miss is informative rather than a failure. XPH (equal-weight, generics-heavy pharma) merged into XBI; PPH (cap-weighted big pharma) stayed on its own. Big pharma really is a different bet from biotech. Equal-weight pharma is not.

02 — Redundancy75 candidates collapse to 45 distinct bets

Complete-linkage clustering on residual |ρ| ≥ 0.70 — complete, not single, or semiconductors chain to software to cloud to fintech through a run of merely adjacent pairs and the whole thing becomes one bet.

25 of the 45 survived as singletons, so the library was not simply padded. The merges the method found that I had not predicted are the interesting ones:

03 — The ceilingThe curve elbows immediately, then stops

This is the result the study exists to produce. Coverage rises steeply for the first ten ETFs and then flattens against a hard ceiling that more ETFs cannot lift.

Read against the fit distribution, the ceiling is not a quirk of the greedy selection — it is where the stocks simply run out. Most of the universe has no good match at any basis size.

04 — Where it worksCompression finds rates and commodities, not products

Almost all of the coverage comes from groups where the company is a proxy for a rate or a commodity. A regional bank is an interest-rate position with a logo; a gold miner is levered gold.

The motivating example is the case that fails. "Fifteen highly correlated semiconductor stocks" was the premise for this whole investigation. SMH is the best match for 93 stocks — but only 23 clear R² ≥ 0.3, and their median R² is 0.17. Semiconductor stocks are not one bet on this data. AMD, Broadcom and Micron have genuinely different businesses, and the ETF captures about a sixth of what moves them once the market is removed.

05 — MomentumInside the 28%, the hierarchy genuinely works

This is the part of the result that is positive, and it is worth stating precisely because it is also the part most easily over-read.

So an ETF-first screen would successfully find where most leaders are — within the 28% of the universe it can see at all. Applied to the whole universe it is blind to the other 72%, which includes most of technology and most of health care.

06 — BaselineThe product already does this, better

The change I made to the proposed plan, and the one that decides the answer: the baseline is not "no grouping". Cali already clusters its entire universe by correlation.

The correlation grouping covers roughly twice as many names at an order of magnitude finer granularity, and it is derived from the stocks themselves rather than from an editorial list of ETFs somebody has to maintain. An ETF layer would replace a finer instrument with a coarser one.

07 — NextWhat I would test, and what I would not

  • Attack the real complaint directly. "The Top 100 is full of the same trade" is measurable today: how many of the top 100 share a correlation group? If that number is small the problem is smaller than assumed; if large, the fix is presenting the existing groups better, not adding an ETF layer.
  • Split the regime. Re-run on 2023–24 against 2025–26. If KRE's 125-stock absorption halves, the compression is a rate-cycle artefact rather than a structural fact — 756 sessions covers one cycle.
  • Do groups predict? Whether last month's group momentum beats a member's own at predicting next month's return is the only test that would justify ranking groups before stocks. This study does not answer it, and nothing here should be read as if it did.
What is worth keeping. A dozen ETFs summarise the rate-sensitive and commodity-linked block well. That is a real finding, and the honest framing is not "ETFs replace stocks" but "a dozen ETFs are a good context panel for the third of the universe that is a macro proxy" — which is close to what ETF River already shows.
